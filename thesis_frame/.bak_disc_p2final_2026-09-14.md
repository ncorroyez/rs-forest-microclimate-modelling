# General Discussion

*(Draft, 2026-08-13; revised 2026-08-18 after the five-way cross-check against the
Introduction, Chapters 1–3 and Appendix G. Evidence for the perspectives in Appendix G.
Figures and cross-references to be renumbered at thesis assembly.)*

## D.1 Three questions, answered in order

This thesis asked how the understory microclimate of temperate deciduous forests can be
modelled from remote sensing, given that each sensor sees only part of the canopy. The General
Introduction split that problem into three questions to be addressed in order, and each answer
narrows the next question.

Chapter 1 answered the attribution question. Leaf quantity, read as leaf area
and cover together, governs the realised ΔTmax buffering, and both levers
steepen as the canopy closes. In the most open stands every effect falls below
0.03 °C, too small to rank. The vertical arrangement of foliage is second
order. Outside those open stands the real-versus-uniform contrast stays at or
below the cover step, and its realised effect is about 0.05 °C. Chapter 1
carries no field validation of its own. Chapter 3 supplies it. Over the 53
loggers the simulated and observed buffering agree at *r* = 0.79 on the
coupling slope and *r* = 0.63 on the daytime offset. The model therefore
recovers the ordering of the plots on the buffering gradient better than the
amplitude of their offsets. If quantity governs the buffering, a
two-dimensional sensor might suffice. Chapter 1 quantified the accuracy it
must reach. A uniform 23 % reduction of the retrieved leaf area, the shift
implied by the optimised extinction coefficient, moves the simulated coupling
by more than Chapter 3 resolves against the loggers. An optical proxy must
therefore keep its leaf-area error well below 23 %, and that is the regime in
which optical retrievals saturate.

Chapter 2 answered the consistency question. Sentinel-2 LAI saturates against the LiDAR reference in dense canopy, with a ceiling near 6–7 m² m⁻² where LiDAR reaches 10–15, and overshoots it in sparse canopy. The divergence is one of dynamic range rather than of central tendency. Regression slopes remain well below unity (0.25–0.80 across configurations) after every correction. The site means are comparable once the LiDAR extinction coefficient is set to *k* = 0.65, the value adopted for inter-sensor work. Chapter 1 keeps *k* = 0.5. The LiDAR integration depth at which the two products agree best, which we termed the effective optical depth d_opt, is 6–10 m (7 m across sites), against canopy tops of 17–32 m. It is an empirical proxy for the canopy thickness that dominates the Sentinel-2 signal, not a measured penetration depth. The third component was horizontal. Agreement declined with within-pixel canopy-height heterogeneity at all three sites, and the residual errors after both corrections remained largest where canopy tops are discontinuous. A single fixed d_opt is therefore an averaging that holds for closed, homogeneous canopies, and it marks the edge of the domain over which consistency was established. The chapter claimed consistency rather than accuracy, and it
ended by proposing the optimised product as an operational input for
microclimate modelling. Chapter 3 inherited that proposal, together with the question the General Introduction had left open: whether better agreement in leaf
area propagates into better simulated microclimate.

Chapter 3 answered the forcing question by testing that proposal. The best
product depended on the canopy archetype. In dense canopy Sentinel-2
saturates: only the products carrying the LiDAR magnitude rank the plots
(coupling slope *r* = 0.72 for LiDAR, 0.68 for the combination, 0.32 for raw
Sentinel-2). In mid-density canopy the two cannot be separated on 12 plots: Sentinel-2 ranks at *r* = 0.60 against 0.67 on the slope and 0.75 against 0.69 on the daytime offset, both differences including zero. In the open gaps the LiDAR leaf area is near zero, which
the point cloud confirms, while Sentinel-2 retrieves 4 to 5 m² m⁻²; LiDAR is
the reliable product there. Pooled over all plots, Sentinel-2 appears better
(*r* = 0.86 against 0.79); the advantage is a composition effect of the
archetypes. The dense-canopy conclusion is model-free (within-dense *r* =
−0.60) and survives the robustness checks. The LiDAR leaf area is invariant to
point density, down to a tenth of the native sampling (Spearman *ρ* ≥ 0.99).
Sentinel-2 adds its seasonality where the canopy does not saturate.

## D.2 The canopy-density gradient across the three chapters

The three chapters describe one canopy-density gradient. Chapter 1 finds the leaf-quantity lever steepest in its densest archetype. Chapter 2 places the optical saturation near a 6–7 m² m⁻² ceiling, reached where the LiDAR estimate still climbs to 10–15. Chapter 3 shows that the Sentinel-2 ranking deficit grows with the fraction of leaf area below the effective optical depth (*r* = −0.71 across the dense archetypes). The three chapters do not share one extinction coefficient (*k* = 0.5 in Chapter 1, 0.65 in Chapters 2 and 3), so the numerical alignment should not be over-read. The mechanism is shared: radiation extinction accumulates with leaf area, so an optical sensor cannot see through the layers that buffer the understory most. In the dense Blois plots most of the leaf area sits below the effective optical depth and is invisible from above, so the Sentinel-2 magnitude saturates in the stands where the buffering is largest. Independent voxel-based simulations support this mechanism: Sentinel-2 near-infrared reflectance responds to the vertical position of the foliage density peak, and this sensitivity weakens as the foliage spreads vertically [@fujiwaraEvaluatingSensitivitySentinel22026].

The Chapter 1 sensitivities show the same geometry. Its leaf-area step is
steepest in the densest archetype, and the two directions are not mirror
images there (0.205 against 0.172 °C), so the effect steepens as the canopy
closes. The cover step is steeper still, 0.265 °C per 10 points, and Chapter 1
does not rank the two against each other. That geometry belongs to the
sensitivities, not to the model's skill. The simulation ranks the dense and
the sparse halves of the 53 loggers equally well, at *r* = 0.78 and 0.82 on
ΔTmax. Chapter 3 revises the reading of the open plots. The open plots are
genuine gaps. Their LiDAR point clouds are almost all ground returns, so the
near-zero LiDAR leaf area is correct. Sentinel-2 retrieves 4 to 5 m² m⁻² of a
leaf area that is not there. The Sentinel-2 signal in the open therefore
reflects the understory and soil background, not a tree canopy, and Sentinel-2
is the least reliable product there. Where the canopy is this sparse the
simulated microclimate is only weakly sensitive to the forcing, so the open
plots contribute little to the choice of sensor.

## D.3 Inter-sensor consistency versus microclimate skill

The most consequential cross-chapter result is that inter-sensor consistency
and microclimate skill are two objectives, and optimising the first can
degrade the second. Accuracy here means fidelity to the retrieval objective,
inter-sensor consistency (§D.5); with that gloss, The General Introduction
posed it as an open, empirical question, and the chapters answered it three
times. First, Chapter 1 showed that the coupling is calibrated to a scale. A
uniform 23 % reduction of the LiDAR leaf area moves the simulated coupling by
more than Chapter 3 resolves against the loggers. An optical proxy must
therefore stay well below that error. That is the direction and order of the
*k* = 0.65 rescaling that maximises inter-sensor consistency in Chapter 2.
Second, Chapter 2 produced the most inter-sensor-consistent retrieval of the
thesis, a site-specific PROSAIL configuration. It reduces the RMSE against the
depth-truncated LiDAR LAI by 1.2 m² m⁻² on average and recentres the bias at
two sites (a +0.7 m² m⁻² residual remains at Blois). At Blois, the site of
Chapter 3, the selected LAI prior is the truncated LiDAR distribution itself,
so the product was tuned to the upper 6 m of the canopy. Third, Chapter 3
found that this same product drives the dense-canopy microclimate worse than
the untuned retrieval it was meant to improve on (dense coupling slope *r* =
0.09 against 0.32). In the model-free test its correlation with the observed
buffering is sign-inverted (*r* = +0.14, where LiDAR gives −0.60). The same
geometry sets the dense deficit directly: the Sentinel-2 ranking deficit grows
with the fraction of leaf area buried below the effective optical depth (*r* =
−0.71 across the dense archetypes).

The optimisation objective determines the outcome. Inter-sensor consistency
and functional fidelity are two objective functions, and a retrieval tuned to
match a saturating signal inherits the saturation. Chapter 2 proposed the
optimised product as "an operational input" for microclimate modelling.
Chapter 3 tested the proposal: in dense canopy the forest-tuned product is a
worse driver than the raw retrieval (coupling slope *r* = 0.09 against 0.32).
The two chapters are consistent. Chapter 2 built the best product the optical
route allows. Chapter 3 shows that the constraint is upstream of the tuning,
in the retrieved per-pixel leaf area. A random forest on the raw reflectance
recovers dense ranking that the retrieval discards, at ΔT~max~ *R*² = 0.31 on
the ten bands and 0.48 on the three the inversion reads. The retrieved leaf
area gives 0.02 there and LiDAR 0.42.

## D.4 The LiDAR contribution: magnitude rather than vertical detail

Two statements in this thesis look contradictory until a distinction is made.
Chapter 1 concludes that the vertical detail of ALS contributes little to the
realised buffering signal. Replacing each real profile by its archetype mean
moves ΔT~max~ by 0.01 to 0.07 °C, and the realised profile effect is about
0.05 °C. A model-free test on the 53 loggers agrees. Adding the vertical
profile to leaf area, cover and height raises the explained variance in the
observed offset by 0.013 (permutation *p* = 0.034), and by 0.003 on the
coupling slope (*p* = 0.26). Maximum height stays within 0.001 °C per metre of
zero in every archetype, its widest interval bound being 0.003 °C. That figure
is a lower bound, since the aerodynamic pathway is held fixed. The direction
the General Introduction hypothesised, that foliage concentrated high in the
canopy buffers more, holds over most of the density range but as a
second-order lever, and reverses at the dense end. Chapter 3 concludes that
LiDAR is indispensable in dense canopy. Both hold, because they concern
different properties of the instrument. The microclimate model needs the LiDAR
magnitude because it does not saturate: the optical retrieval loses the
between-plot ranking throughout the dense stratum, well before its 6–7 m² m⁻²
ceiling. The three-dimensional detail of ALS serves here mostly to measure
leaf quantity without saturating. Canopy height plays a third role: negligible
as a physical lever, it is a serviceable classifier of the density regime that
organises Chapter 3, cheap to obtain wall-to-wall
[@schwartzFORMSForestMultiple2023; @langHighresolutionCanopyHeight2022;
@potapovMappingGlobalForest2021].

The distinction also closes the question the General Introduction inherited
from @bouwenInteractionsEntreStructure. That study attributed part of its
under-buffering to the parametric beta profile it had to assume and recommended
substituting measured LiDAR profiles. We made the substitution, in a deciduous
canopy, and isolated the profile at matched leaf area and height: its realised
contribution is small, and the warm bias and compressed amplitude persist
unchanged (§D.6). The assumed profile was not the cause of the under-buffering;
the one-dimensional column is.

## D.5 Working without ground truth

No field LAI was used in this thesis, at any site, in any chapter, and none
concurrent with the acquisitions was available. The argumentative structure
was built to survive this. Three mutually independent supports replace the
missing reference. The first is mechanistic attribution (Chapter 1), its
safeguards were physical parameters fitted to no microclimate measurement, and
perturbations centred on each plot's own measured canopy. The second is
inter-sensor consistency (Chapter 2), which quantifies agreement between two
imperfect instruments without electing either as truth. The reference
instruments themselves disagree in closed forests
[@woodgateUnderstandingVariabilityGroundbased2015], so a field campaign would
have relocated the problem rather than solved it. The third is functional
constraint (Chapter 3), in which 53 temperature loggers, independent of both
the model and the LAI retrievals, judge LAI products by what they are for.
Each support has a named limitation. The attribution's ranking is corroborated
model-free, but its magnitudes are model-internal, and only the ranking was
claimed to transfer. The consistency analysis carries a bounded prior
circularity (≤ 17 % of the reported RMSE reduction), an unbounded scoring
circularity, and a reference-driven d_opt selection. The constraint is
single-site, and its spatially non-independent loggers make the reported *R*²
optimistic. The thesis therefore claims rankings and regime assignments, not
calibrated effect sizes. It reports ranking, dispersion, and bias separately
throughout, with a model-free leaf-area test as a fourth axis in Chapter 3.
The axes disagree too often to be summarised by one number. The same
discipline applies to the vapour-pressure-deficit offsets that Chapter 1
computed: they are model-internal, unvalidated by the loggers, and were
carried only as a robustness check on the temperature result.

The point clouds settle one discrepancy between chapters directly. On the open plots the LiDAR leaf area differs by up to a factor of three between the archetype clustering of Chapter 1 and the forcing of Chapter 3. The two handle the near-ground returns differently. The most open plots are almost all ground returns, so the near-zero Chapter 3 value is correct and the Chapter 1 clustering overestimates leaf area there. The archetype assignment is unaffected, since the eight open plots are the eight sparsest by either measure, so only the open-canopy magnitude differs, not the ranking.

## D.6 Simulation defects common to all forcings

A recurring pattern across Chapters 1 and 3 sets the agenda for what follows
this thesis. However the canopy is described, the simulation shares four
defects. A warm bias survives every forcing tested, from +0.30 °C under the
fixed LiDAR leaf area to +0.72 °C under the forest-tuned Sentinel-2 retrieval.
The between-plot amplitude is compressed, to dispersion ratios at or below
0.23 in Chapter 3. The nighttime warm bias is +0.14 to +0.60 °C in Chapter 3,
which Chapter 1 did not examine. Skill degrades on the hottest days, where
predictions matter most. These defects move together when the forcing changes,
but they do not close. Every forcing change tested so far moved leaf area
down, a 23 % cut in Chapter 1 and an optical truncation in Chapter 3, and both
worsened them. No chapter tested a higher leaf-area scale (*k* < 0.5, or a
clumping correction), the direction that could reduce the warm bias inside the
present column. Subject to that untested direction, the margin of progress has
shifted from the forcing to the model. The one-dimensional column omits the
sub-pixel gaps that structure the residual, together with edge effects,
lateral advection, and cold-air drainage [@reekForestEdgesAre2025]. The
omission is largest on the amplifying tail of the gradient, the open plots
that run warmer than the open air. Part of the measured gap there is protocol
rather than physics: non-aspirated shields read warm in open, sunlit, low-wind
stands [@terandoAdHocInstrumentation2017], and the loggers sit at 1 m against
a 1.5 m reference.

The nighttime side deserves one further observation, because an exploratory
analysis of the logger archive (Appendix G, §G.8) changes how we read it.
Nighttime ΔTmin couples to LiDAR leaf area almost as strongly as daytime
ΔTmax, with the opposite sign (*r* = +0.83 against −0.89). Closed canopy holds
the night at the reference temperature while open stands fall 2 to 4 °C below
it. That mirrors the daytime pattern, a bilateral amplitude compression under
one structural control. Chapter 3 treated the night as a sensor-agnostic
regime: the daytime reversal dissolves, both products rank the plots
comparably (pooled ΔTmin *R*² 0.62–0.69), and the model runs +0.1–0.6 °C warm.
The correlation above suggests that the missing piece is amplitude and bias
rather than ranking. Cold-air pooling and topographic position remain
unexamined covariates of both the observation and its macro reference, which
is the forcing file itself. We present this as a consistency check against the
mechanistic attribution rather than an attribution in its own right. An
observational correlation cannot override the attribution the perturbation
design isolates. The collinearity trap that motivated Chapter 1 applies to our
follow-up analyses as much as to the literature. The ecological reading is
also two-sided, since warmer nights under dense canopy do not relieve
accumulated water stress. The nighttime regime is the main question this
thesis leaves open.

## D.7 Perspectives

### D.7.1 Spaceborne LiDAR as the missing temporal dimension

The General Introduction set GEDI aside on the grounds that its sampling
geometry and geolocation uncertainty keep it short of what plot-scale
mechanistic modelling requires. That judgement stands, and our exploratory analysis confirms its core (Appendix G). GEDI's dense-canopy skill is real at its footprints but does not regress beyond them, so it cannot replace ALS as a per-plot structural source. GEDI can meet a weaker requirement, one this thesis left unmet. Every
LiDAR quantity in the preceding chapters is a summer snapshot; no LiDAR truth
existed outside that window. GEDI samples year-round [@dubayahGlobalEcosystemDynamics2020] and supplies exactly that. Monthly composites at constant ALS-measured structure trace a full deciduous cycle, with a winter floor that is plausibly wood and branch area, so the sensor separates leaf from wood by season. GEDI canopy-cover profiles show the same seasonality elsewhere, concentrated in the 10 to 25 m layers of broadleaved stands and absent from coniferous ones [@liuInvestigatingAssociationSeasonal2024]. Their seasonal amplitude still ranks canopy density where the summer optical signal is flat. Their end of season falls 25 to 34 days later than the Sentinel-2 series, replicating at one site the continental gap reported by @cotrina-sanchezPhenologyEuropeanForests2026. Chapter 3 confined its logger validation to summer, but the archive extends through autumn. We re-ran the Chapter 3 machinery over October and November, with the autumn limb of the seasonal series constrained by GEDI. That improved the simulated microclimate against those loggers (47 series plots; autumn ΔRMSE −0.08 °C, Δ*R*² +0.19). Composing the GEDI correction with the
Chapter 3 combination gave the best forcing this thesis has evaluated, ahead of the combination
alone in both summer and autumn (Appendix G, §G.5). Chapter 3 cannot itself decide that gain.
On a full-leaf summer plateau the combination carries almost no seasonal shape and is close to
the fixed LiDAR forcing by construction, so only the autumn limb tests a seasonal correction. These are exploratory, single-site results. The corrected series was reached through sequential variants scored on the same logger network, so the confidence intervals are conditional on that selection. We weight the large, pre-specified effects, September and autumn. The three sensors divide the roles. ALS supplies the magnitude and the dense-canopy ranking. Sentinel-2 supplies the open-canopy ranking and the per-plot leaf-on timing, which a site-level GEDI shape cannot take over, although Chapter 3 found that this timing adds little on the summer plateau. GEDI supplies the seasonal constraint at leaf-out and senescence, where optical senescence runs early [@oliveiraUpperCanopyUnderstory2025; @soudaniEvaluationOnsetGreenup2008]. The late GEDI end of season mixes true leaves, marcescent leaves (typical of *Quercus* and *Fagus*), and exposed wood. For radiation and microclimate this structural signal is the relevant one, as the logger test supports. GEDI's dense-canopy skill does not regress beyond its footprints, so it complements rather than replaces the wall-to-wall products of Chapters 2 and 3 [@schwartzFORMSForestMultiple2023]. Within about 150 m of a power-beam footprint, the direct GEDI measurement recovers 80 to 90 % of the ALS microclimate correlation (*n* = 11–18 plots). The growing footprint archive can therefore serve as local truth between airborne campaigns, at least in lowland broadleaf stands. At the montane site the footprint-level agreement with ALS is itself weak (*r* = 0.13–0.22, September only, conifer admixture; Appendix G, §G.9), so no such check is available there. That boundary is part of the perspective.

### D.7.2 An extreme-year stress test

Whether structural buffering persists under the climate it is supposed to
mitigate is the operational premise of mapping forest microrefugia
[@lenoirClimaticMicrorefugiaAnthropogenic2017;
@defrenneGlobalBufferingTemperatures2019;
@davisMicroclimaticBufferingForests2019]. The logger network stayed active
through the record June and July 2022 heatwaves, and the answer at this site
is encouraging on the axis that carries the evidence (Appendix G, §G.6).

The observed LAI-buffering coupling held on the hottest days: *r* = −0.88 in
both years' hot windows, against −0.92 over all of summer 2021. The mean
buffering slope did not collapse either (0.93 on the hottest 2022 days against
0.86 on the hottest 2021 days; Appendix G, Table G4). GEDI composites showed
the 2022 canopy itself intact. The mean offset was deeper on hot days: −1.22 °C on the hottest 2022 days, against −1.13 °C on the hottest 2021 days and
−0.73 °C over all of summer 2021. That deepening is the expected arithmetic of
a sub-unity slope under hotter skies, not evidence of adaptation. The coupling
itself did not move.

The scenario ranking also transferred out of its calibration year, with the
fusion products keeping the lead. Part of the drought literature reports
buffering that weakens as soils dry [@greiserHigherSoilMoisture2024]. A
lowland oak forest on deep soils in one exceptional year nuances that
expectation rather than overturning it. The model's warm bias grew with heat
severity (+1.16 °C), so the stress test supports the forcing and confirms the
model bias of §D.6.

### D.7.3 The vernal window and the direction of the association

Two small analyses close the loop between phenology and microclimate that
recent work has opened from the other side [@wuCanopyStructureRegulates2024].
Layer-resolved GEDI profiles show the understory greening up about a week
before the overstory, a measured vernal window. Crossing per-plot green-up dates with the plots' own spring temperatures then quantifies its thermal consequence. Later-leafing plots accumulate a warmer understory spring (*r* = +0.55, partialled on summer leaf area +0.40; *n* = 33). Green-up was dated by the same smoothed half-amplitude threshold as Appendix G, Table G1. The
reciprocal association is weaker: winter understory temperature relates to
green-up mostly through leaf area (*r* = +0.40, partial +0.19; *n* = 35;
Appendix G, §G.7). The design is cross-sectional, and April canopy openness, species composition, and topographic position are not controlled. We therefore state the result as an asymmetry of associations. At stand scale, in one year, the spring understory temperature follows phenology more closely than winter temperature anticipates it. The asymmetry is testable further with the same
archive, and it matters for any attempt to force spring microclimate from
remotely sensed leaf-out dates.

### D.7.4 The remaining avenues

The remaining avenues are listed in decreasing order of readiness. The
first is dimensional. §D.6 locates the next margin of progress in the column itself. The archetype-dependent forcing of Chapter 3 should next be evaluated in the coupled canopy-atmosphere formulations named in the General Introduction (Grulois, 2026, PhD thesis, ISPA Bordeaux, coupled ARPS-MuSICA; @vandewalleForEdgeClimV103D2026), in the edges and gaps a column cannot see. Sequential data assimilation of the
Sentinel-2 stream remains the one untested fusion family, and it combines
naturally with a GEDI-tested phenology. A second, spectral avenue is on the retrieval side. Chapter 3 ran the Sentinel-2 inversion on the 10 m grid to match the microclimate resolution, which resamples the red-edge and short-wave-infrared bands from their native 20 m. Those bands carry much of the leaf-area information least affected by saturation. A retrieval at the native 20 m resolution, super-resolved back to
10 m, could reduce the dense-canopy saturation that drives the choice of sensor. No such avenue is needed in open canopy. Chapter 3 found the open plots to be genuine gaps: the Sentinel-2 signal there is understory and soil background (not a sub-2 m canopy layer hidden by the LiDAR height filter).
Dedicated field LAI campaigns, with the standardised protocols their own
uncertainties demand [@woodgateUnderstandingVariabilityGroundbased2015],
would convert consistency statements into accuracy statements. Conifer and
mixed stands are the stated boundary of every chapter, and the montane site
of this thesis, where the height gate and GEDI both weaken, is the natural
stress case. One practical note for replication. Subsampling the logger network shows that 15 to 20 well-spread loggers reproduce the slope-LAI coupling within this network (Appendix G, §G.8). Whether that transfers to another site is untested, but the validation design of this thesis looks reproducible at modest cost.

## D.8 Concluding remarks

This thesis produces the specification a wall-to-wall microclimate map would be built on. The
General Introduction set that boundary in advance. Leaf quantity is the controlling structural
variable, and it has to be measured without saturation where the buffering is deepest. That
still means airborne LiDAR wherever the canopy is dense. Which leaf-area source to trust then
depends on the canopy archetype. The seasonal shape has to be constrained at leaf-out and
senescence, outside the summer window Chapter 3 tested. A simulation bias remains that no
forcing repairs, and that is where the next methodological work belongs.

The specification is falsifiable and cheap to test elsewhere: a network of 15 to 20 loggers and
one summer. Its first two exploratory trials, one out-of-year (the 2022 heatwaves) and one
against an independent sensor (the GEDI-corrected phenology), both came back positive.
