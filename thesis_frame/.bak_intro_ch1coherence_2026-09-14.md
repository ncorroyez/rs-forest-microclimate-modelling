# 1. General Introduction

## 1.1 The ecological importance of forest microclimates in a changing climate

### 1.1.1 Climate change and the increasing frequency of extreme phenomena

Climate change raises mean temperature, and, more consequentially for living systems, it raises
the frequency and severity of extremes. The state of the climate system and the human influence on it are now tracked and updated annually against the methods of the IPCC's Sixth Assessment. The Earth's energy imbalance has more than doubled relative to the late twentieth century [@forsterIndicatorsGlobalClimate2026]. What that heating does to living systems is felt less through the shift in the annual mean than through the hot days, heatwaves, and compound heat and drought episodes superimposed on it. Those push organisms past physiological thresholds a slowly warming mean never reaches. Under two degrees of mean warming, a forest
acquires more days on which leaf temperature approaches the threshold for photosynthetic
damage. It also acquires more nights that fail to relieve the accumulated water deficit, and
longer runs of both in succession.

The consequences reach forests through damage as much as through physiology. Extreme drought leaves a measurable imprint on the vitality of individual trees, modulated by their size and their neighbourhood [@riederTreeSizeNeighbourhood2026]. At stand scale, drought-induced dieback and severe wildfire have intensified over recent decades [@cassellWidespreadSevereWildfires2019], and European disturbance regimes are projected to intensify further under continued warming [@grunigClimateChangeWill2026]. This matters here because of a circularity. Disturbance removes
canopy, and canopy is the physical agent that attenuates the extremes beneath it, so canopy lost to one heat and drought episode cannot buffer the next. Where the canopy is removed outright the buffering does not return quickly: boreal stands need about thirty years after a clear-cut before the sub-canopy temperature is buffered as strongly as in mature forest [@starckSlowRecoveryMicroclimate2025].

The relevant question for forest ecology is how hot it gets on the worst days, and where.
Near-ground temperature responds to stand structure most strongly in the warm extremes, and average and minimum temperatures differ far less between cut and uncut stands [@potterImpactForestStructure2001]. The daily maximum is therefore where canopy structure leaves its clearest thermal signature. The where is the half most often left unanswered. Macroclimatic
projections are produced on grids of a kilometre or coarser, whereas the conditions that
determine whether a seedling survives a heatwave vary over metres.

### 1.1.2 The forest canopy as a physical modifier of the sub-canopy climate

Forests modify the regional climate they sit in. The canopy intercepts radiation, slows
turbulent exchange, and transpires, so the air beneath it is partly decoupled from the free
atmosphere above [@geigerClimateGround1995; @defrenneGlobalBufferingTemperatures2019]. To
quantify that decoupling we use a single operational metric throughout this thesis: the canopy
temperature offset, ΔTmax = Tmicro − Tmacro, the difference between the sub-canopy and the
above-canopy daily maximum temperature. We focus on the daily maximum because it is the
component most relevant to the heat extremes of Section 1.1.1. A negative ΔTmax means the
understory is cooler than the open air, that is, microclimatic buffering; a positive ΔTmax
means it is warmer, that is, amplification.

Across biomes, closed canopies buffer: they cool hot daytime maxima and warm cold nights, compressing
the temperature range experienced below [@defrenneGlobalBufferingTemperatures2019;
@zellwegerForestMicroclimateDynamics2020]. The effect is asymmetric between day and night, because
its daytime and nighttime causes differ. During the day, foliage intercepts shortwave radiation
before it can heat the forest floor, so the understory stays cooler than the open. At night, the same
foliage restricts the escape of longwave radiation to a cold sky, so the understory stays warmer.
Both effects narrow the diurnal range, but only the daytime one attenuates heat extremes, so ΔTmax rather than the mean or minimum offset is the metric of interest here. Old-growth stands,
with deep and continuous canopies, buffer most strongly [@freySpatialModelsReveal2016], and
maintaining canopy cover is among the few management options that demonstrably preserve this capacity
under future climate [@delombaerdeMaintainingForestCover2022].

Buffering is not universal. Its sign and strength depend on canopy structure, and where the canopy
is sparse or open the offset can reverse, so that the understory overheats relative to the open
air [@kovacsStandStructuralDrivers2017; @zellwegerSeasonalDriversUnderstorey2019]. Structural heterogeneity acts in the same direction, widening the diurnal range in stands whose canopy is uneven rather than uniformly closed [@ehbrechtEffectsStructuralHeterogeneity2019]. Severe disturbance can push a stand from the buffering to the amplifying side entirely [@atkinsEffectsForestStructural2023]. Species composition and stand density modulate the effect
across latitudes, with conifer and broadleaf canopies buffering differently [@diaz-calafatBroadleavesConifersEffect2023]. In a tree-diversity experiment the effect of species mixing on the offset was largely indirect, acting through the canopy cover a mixture develops [@zhangTreeSpeciesMixing2022]. Management leaves its own signature on the heterogeneity of buffering within and between stands [@mengeImpactsForestManagement2023]. Forest
edges add a further gradient, over which sub-canopy conditions converge towards the open within a few tens of metres [@meeussenMicroclimaticEdgetointeriorGradients2021].

This context-dependent amplification is a documented field phenomenon, observed independently
of any model, though radiative overheating of the sensors themselves in open plots can
complicate the measurement [@grilUsingAirborneLiDAR2023]. Establishing it on observational
grounds matters, because amplification is mistaken for an artifact wherever it later appears in
a simulation. Buffering also varies through time, weakening as soils dry
[@greiserHigherSoilMoisture2024] and tracking the phenological cycle of leaf area through the
season [@zellwegerSeasonalDriversUnderstorey2019].

Two further qualifications bound what this thesis claims. First, temperature is not the only
variable buffered. The canopy modifies atmospheric humidity, and with it vapour pressure
deficit, at least as strongly as it modifies temperature, and vapour pressure deficit is more
proximate to plant water stress [@burtonDownscalingVaporPressure2024]. We lead on temperature
because it is what the logger networks record, and therefore the only quantity against which
simulations can be validated here. Where a vapour-pressure-deficit offset is computed, it
remains model-internal for want of a matching observation. Second, canopy structure is not the
only driver. Topography redistributes radiation and cold air, soil moisture modulates the
evaporative term, and the two interact with structure rather than adding to it
[@juckerCanopyStructureTopography2018; @davisMicroclimaticBufferingForests2019]. The
microclimate work of this thesis is conducted at a lowland site of low relief, so structure can
be read with less topographic confounding than at a site of strong relief.

### 1.1.3 Why fine-scale temperature variability matters

Fine-scale variability is the climate understory organisms experience. Sub-canopy temperature governs the composition and dynamics of understory plant
communities, and buffered forests can slow the thermophilisation of their flora under macroclimate
warming [@defrenneMicroclimateModeratesPlant2013; @depauwForestUnderstoreyCommunities2022]. When species distributions are re-examined against microclimatic rather than macroclimatic temperatures, the thermal niches inferred for forest plants shift by several degrees [@haesenMicroclimateRevealsTrue2023]. Much of the published climate-change vulnerability of forest understory species has therefore been estimated against the wrong temperature. Vascular plants and bryophytes differ in
their affinity for buffered conditions, so the microclimate filters communities rather than
shifting them wholesale [@grilAffinityVascularPlants2024].

Regeneration is the second process at stake, and it is the one most directly tied to canopy
structure. Seedling survival and early growth respond to the temperature and moisture of the first
metre above the ground, not to the conditions logged at a weather station kilometres away
[@meeussenInitialOakRegeneration2022; @vonarxMicroclimateForestsVarying2013]. Juveniles and adults of
the same species do not experience the same climate, and the thermal gap between them has widened
over recent decades [@caronThermalDifferencesJuveniles2021]. Since the juvenile stage occupies the sub-canopy layer that the overstory buffers, the demographic bottleneck of temperate forests is set in the sub-canopy layer. Fauna, too, exploit the structure: canopy-derived
thermal shelters are measurably used by large mammals during summer heat
[@melinMooseLcesAlces2014].

Under a warming climate, structurally buffered stands can act as thermal microrefugia, pockets
where cool-adapted species persist while the surrounding region warms
[@lenoirClimaticMicrorefugiaAnthropogenic2017; @lenoirUnveilUnseenUsing2022]. Accounting for them
materially changes the velocity at which species must move to track a shifting climate
[@soiferMicroclimatesSlowAlter2026], and microclimatic heterogeneity within a stand is itself
associated with structural complexity and with biodiversity
[@ehbrechtQuantifyingStandStructural2017]. Beyond the species level, the microclimate modulates
ecosystem functioning, mediating part of the effect of plant diversity on process rates
[@beugnonMicroclimateModulationOverlooked2024]. Ignoring the sub-canopy layer therefore biases our
expectations of biodiversity change [@lembrechtsIncorporatingMicroclimateSpecies2019;
@kemppinenMicroclimateImportantPart2024]. Whether such refugia can be located and mapped depends on
our ability to predict the microclimate, not only to describe it in a few places
[@zellwegerForestMicroclimateDynamics2020].

That dependence sets the standard the rest of this work has to meet. A microclimate prediction
is only as good as the canopy description it is given, and the errors that matter are
structured. A structural input that under-reports foliage in dense canopy will make buffered
stands look less buffered than they are. One that fails to register the openness of a gap will
hide the amplification that determines seedling survival. Both mistakes compress the range of
conditions a forest contains, so a model given such an input can fit the observations on
average while misplacing the extremes that decide where a microrefugium lies.

### 1.1.4 Observing microclimate: logger networks and their spatial limitation

The empirical foundation of microclimate ecology is the in-situ temperature logger. Networks of
small sensors, typified by HOBO loggers and coordinated efforts such as SoilTemp, now supply
large, standardised archives of sub-canopy conditions
[@lembrechtsMicroclimaticConditionsAnywhere2020; @lembrechtsGlobalMapsSoil2022]. They are
accurate, they resolve the daily cycle, and every simulation reported in this thesis is
answerable to them [@macleanMeasurementMicroclimate2021].

Deploying them well takes care. A sensor exposed to direct sunlight reads its own radiative
load rather than the air temperature. Shielding, orientation, and mounting height are therefore
part of the measurement, and the bias is worst in the open plots where amplification is
expected [@macleanMeasurementMicroclimate2021; @grilUsingAirborneLiDAR2023]. The placement of
loggers determines which part of the structural gradient is sampled, and a network that
oversamples closed canopy will underestimate the range of conditions present
[@lembrechtsDesigningCountrywideRegional2021]. Dense local networks can be interpolated into
high-resolution grids where the instrumentation effort is sufficient
[@brunaHighresolutionMicroclimaticGrids2026], but the effort scales with area.

The core limitation is simpler than any of these: a logger measures one point. It cannot, on
its own, map how ΔTmax varies continuously across a heterogeneous forest landscape, because the
sensor network is always far sparser than the structural variation it is trying to capture
[@zellwegerAdvancesMicroclimateEcology2019]. A campaign of fifty loggers in a forest of several
thousand hectares samples a vanishing fraction of the structural configurations present, and
the configurations it misses are not random.

Predicting the microclimate between the points is therefore the central problem of this thesis.
It means combining two things: a plot-scale mechanistic understanding of how canopy structure
controls temperature, and continuous remotely sensed descriptions of that structure. Every
sensor imposes a trade-off. Airborne LiDAR resolves three-dimensional structure but only once
and locally, whereas satellite optical imagery is repeated but two-dimensional and prone to
saturation. The three research chapters address it in turn.

## 1.2 Drivers of forest microclimate and the role of canopy structure

### 1.2.1 Linking three-dimensional vegetation structure to sub-canopy microclimate

Buffering is an energy-balance phenomenon before it is an ecological one. What reaches the forest
floor, and what the sub-canopy air does with it, is set by four coupled processes, each of which
depends on where the foliage sits.

The first is shortwave interception. Solar radiation is attenuated as it passes through the canopy,
approximately exponentially with cumulative leaf area, so the energy available to heat the understory
falls as the canopy thickens. The rate of that attenuation is governed by an extinction coefficient, which is not a universal constant. It depends on leaf inclination, on solar zenith angle, and on how foliage clumps rather than distributing itself randomly [@baldocchiSolarRadiationOak1984; @campbellExtinctionCoefficientsRadiation1986]. Two canopies with
identical leaf area but different leaf angle distributions therefore transmit different amounts of
light. The second process is the longwave exchange that partly reverses the first at night. Foliage emits downward and screens the understory from a cold sky, so closed stands are warmer than the open after dark. The buffering of minima and of maxima are therefore physically distinct phenomena rather than two views of one.

The third is turbulent transfer. Foliage exerts aerodynamic drag, so wind speed decays with depth
into the canopy and exchange with the atmosphere above weakens, leaving the sub-canopy air partly
insulated from whatever the free atmosphere is doing [@geigerClimateGround1995]. The consequence is
not uniformly cooling. Weak mixing insulates the understory from warm air aloft, but it equally traps whatever heat is generated below. A sparse canopy that intercepts little radiation while still suppressing ventilation can therefore amplify rather than buffer. Representing this
exchange correctly within and just above the canopy has proved one of the harder problems in land
surface modelling [@bonanModelingCanopyinducedTurbulence2018].

The fourth term is latent heat. Every unit of energy consumed by evaporation is a unit that
does not warm the air. Canopy transpiration is only part of it: the understory vegetation and
the soil surface evaporate as well, and where soil water remains available that flux removes
part of the daytime energy from the sensible-heat budget. Transpiration in temperate oak stands
tracks leaf area closely and varies strongly between years with water availability
[@bredaIntraInterannualVariations1996], and understory leaf area is itself a non-negligible and
poorly measured quantity [@georgeMethodComparisonIndirect2021]. Buffering consequently weakens
as the local water balance dries, so the same canopy buffers less in a drought year than in a
wet one [@davisMicroclimaticBufferingForests2019; @greiserHigherSoilMoisture2024].

Two smaller terms close the balance. Heat conducted into and out of the soil buffers the near-ground air on its own account, storing energy during the day and releasing it at night. Soil and air offsets are therefore correlated but not interchangeable, and logger networks increasingly record both [@lembrechtsGlobalMapsSoil2022]. The energy partitioning is expressed in humidity as much as in temperature. The same latent flux that fails to warm the air does raise its vapour content, so temperature buffering and humidity buffering are two readings of one partitioning [@burtonDownscalingVaporPressure2024].

Canopy models usually collapse the understory layer and the soil surface into a lower boundary
condition rather than resolving them with the detail given to the overstory. Energy a model
fails to route into evaporation it routes into warming the air instead.

Each of these four processes is height-dependent, so structure is three-dimensional rather than
scalar. Radiation is intercepted, drag is exerted and transpiration occurs where the foliage
is, so the canopy heat source sits at whatever height the foliage is concentrated. Detailed
three-dimensional descriptions of old-growth canopies were built to reason about this coupling
between architecture, radiation balance, and gas exchange
[@parkerThreedimensionalStructureOldgrowth2004]. Modelling studies indicate that vertical
canopy architecture affects transpiration and leaf thermoregulation at fixed total leaf area
[@banerjeeEffectVerticalCanopy2018]. Take two stands holding the same total leaf area, one with foliage packed near the top and one with it spread evenly down the profile. They do not present the same vertical arrangement of sources and sinks to the air beneath. Whether that difference
translates into a measurable difference in ΔTmax, and how large it is relative to having more
or fewer leaves, is the question the next section poses.

### 1.2.2 Which dimension of structure? Leaf quantity and vertical arrangement

Canopy structure is usually summarised by the leaf area index (LAI), the one-sided leaf area
per unit ground area [@chenDefiningLeafArea1992]. LAI is attractive because it is a single
number, because it enters radiative transfer directly, and because it correlates strongly with
observed buffering across gradients from closed forest to open plantation
[@hardwickRelationshipLeafArea2015; @vonarxMicroclimateForestsVarying2013]. Even the term
requires care. Most instruments and most remote-sensing retrievals return an effective LAI that
assumes a random distribution of foliage, and so underestimate true leaf area wherever the
canopy is clumped [@chenDefiningLeafArea1992; @chenEvaluationVegetationIndices1996]. Optical
instruments and gap-fraction inversions return plant area, which includes woody elements,
rather than leaf area alone [@brownNearinfraredDigitalHemispherical2024]. Radiative-transfer
schemes differ in whether they expect a one-sided or a two-sided leaf area. Comparisons between
sensors are only meaningful once these conventions are aligned.

The distinctions are not pedantic: a large part of the disagreement between
published leaf area products is definitional before it is physical. Two
consequences run through the thesis. The extinction coefficient of the
airborne inversion is a stated choice rather than a measured property. It
rescales the retrieval by a constant, so the absolute magnitude depends on it
while the ranking between plots does not. No row of this table is a field
measurement of leaf area, so every comparison reported here is between two
model-based estimates.

Leaf area is also not a constant of a stand. It follows a phenological cycle, rising through leaf-out, plateauing through summer, and declining through senescence. In deciduous forest the amplitude of that cycle exceeds most of the between-stand variation observed at any single date [@bredaIntraInterannualVariations1996; @cotrina-sanchezPhenologyEuropeanForests2026]. A single-date
structural description is therefore a snapshot of a moving quantity, which is one reason the temporal
signature of each sensor, developed in Section 1.4, is not a secondary consideration.

LAI is also an integral, and integrals discard information. The same LAI can be realised by a dense canopy covering part of the ground or by a thinner canopy covering all of it. It can equally be realised by foliage concentrated in a narrow upper crown layer or distributed through a deep, multi-layered profile. Several complementary descriptors have therefore been proposed. Fractional cover (fCover)
captures the horizontal dimension, maximum height (*H*max) the depth of the column available for
attenuation, and the vertical profile of leaf area density (LAD) the arrangement within that column.
The idea of characterising a canopy by its foliage profile is old [@macarthurFoliageProfileVertical1969],
but it became routinely measurable only with laser scanning
[@kamoskeLeafAreaDensity2019; @arnqvistRobustProcessingAirborne2020]. A parallel literature has developed indices of structural complexity that compress the three-dimensional point cloud into scalars [@mcelhinnyForestWoodlandStand2005; @beckschaferEnhancedStructuralComplexity2013; @kaneComparisonsFieldLiDARbased2010]. A further strand distinguishes the horizontal from the vertical component of heterogeneity, and the two can act in opposite directions on ecological responses [@carrascoMetricsLidarDerived3D2019].

The literature has not settled which of these dimensions governs the thermal effect. Some studies
report canopy density or cover as the dominant control [@kovacsStandStructuralDrivers2017;
@zellwegerSeasonalDriversUnderstorey2019]. Others find that structural complexity and vertical
heterogeneity carry independent explanatory power for the diurnal temperature range
[@ehbrechtQuantifyingStandStructural2017; @ehbrechtEffectsStructuralHeterogeneity2019]. Others again show the two changing together along disturbance and land-use gradients [@atkinsEffectsForestStructural2023; @juckerCanopyStructureTopography2018]. In boreal stands scanned with terrestrial LiDAR, the total quantity of plant material was not by itself an adequate predictor of the buffering. The number of canopy layers and the density of the understory carried it instead [@starckSlowRecoveryMicroclimate2025]. The same ambiguity
recurs outside the microclimate literature, where canopy structural complexity has been proposed as a
driver of productivity in ways that are equally hard to separate from leaf quantity
[@hardimanRoleCanopyStructural2011; @faheyDefiningSpectrumIntegrative2019].

The disagreement has a structural cause. In real stands these variables covary: tall stands
tend to be dense, dense stands tend to be closed, and closed stands tend to have deep
multi-layered profiles. A study that finds LAI dominant and a study that finds vertical complexity dominant may be reading the same underlying gradient through different predictors. No amount of additional field data collected along that gradient will separate them. We
therefore treat the question as open, in the terms this thesis can test: does the total
quantity of foliage govern sub-canopy buffering, or does its vertical arrangement?

## 1.3 Modelling the forest microclimate

### 1.3.1 Statistical versus mechanistic approaches

Two families of models predict sub-canopy temperature, and they fail in different places.
Statistical, or correlative, models regress the observed offset on structural and topographic
predictors, using linear models, generalised additive models, or machine learning trained on
logger networks. Monthly microclimate models have been built for managed boreal landscapes
[@greiserMonthlyMicroclimateModels2018], and fine-scale warming rates have been mapped from
terrain and canopy predictors [@macleanFinescaleClimateChange2017]. Generalised additive models
interpolate microclimate within stands [@burnettUsingGeneralizedAdditive2019]. Continental
gridded products such as ForestTemp and ForestClim now provide sub-canopy bioclimatic variables
for European forests [@haesenForestTempSubcanopyMicroclimate2021;
@haesenForestClimBioclimaticVariables2023], alongside global maps of soil temperature
[@lembrechtsGlobalMapsSoil2022]. Parsimonious formulations that summarise the micro-macro
relationship by a slope (the buffering slope, as we call it throughout) and an equilibrium term
have proved well suited to comparing sites [@grilSlopeEquilibriumParsimonious2023]. Within the
range of conditions they were fitted on, all of these interpolate well.

Their limitation is the question of Section 1.2.2. When predictors are strongly collinear,
a fitted coefficient is not an effect. Regression coefficients become unstable and their standard errors inflate. The additive analogue in generalised additive models is concurvity, in which one smooth term can be partly reproduced by the others, so the decomposition into individual contributions is not identifiable [@dominiciUseGeneralizedAdditive2002]. Machine learning relocates the problem: permutation-based variable
importance is unstable under predictor correlation, and correlated predictors share importance in
ways that depend on the algorithm rather than on the data [@nicodemusBehaviourRandomForest2010;
@gregoruttiCorrelationVariableImportance2017]. A model of this kind can rank plots correctly
while attributing the ranking to the wrong variable. It is also site-fitted and descriptive, so it extrapolates poorly to structural combinations absent from its training data. It offers no mechanism that would separate the radiative role of leaf quantity from the turbulent role of leaf arrangement.

Mechanistic models take the opposite route. Rather than fitting the offset, they solve the coupled
energy, water, and radiative balance that produces it, so their parameters are in principle
transferable rather than calibrated to one site [@macleanMicroclimcMechanisticModel2021]. The family now includes dedicated microclimate models [@macleanMicroclimcMechanisticModel2021], validated against observations in temperate understories [@brusseMechanisticallyMappingNearsurface2024] and applied to fine-scale variability in boreal forests [@kolstelaRevealingFinescaleVariability2024]. It also includes radiative-transfer approaches to microclimate mapping [@zellwegerMicroclimateMappingUsing2024] and land surface models applied to projections of future forest microclimate [@hesProjectingFutureForest2024]. Mechanistic and empirical approaches
have also been compared directly in degraded tropical forest, with neither dominating on accuracy
alone [@marshMeasuringModellingMicroclimatic2022].

A related difficulty concerns what "accurate" should mean for a microclimate model, and it
governs how any of them can be compared. A model can be judged on how close its simulated offset is to the observed one. It can also be judged on whether it orders plots correctly, or on whether it reproduces the observed spread between them. A model carrying a constant warm bias
may rank plots perfectly while being wrong everywhere in absolute terms. A model that
compresses the simulated range may look accurate on average while being unable to distinguish a
buffered plot from an amplifying one. The ecological use of a microclimate map is usually to find the cool places rather than to predict an exact temperature. This thesis therefore reports ranking, spread and bias separately throughout, never collapsed into one score.

This thesis needs a mechanistic model for controllability, not for accuracy. Because the canopy
description enters as an input rather than as a fitted covariate, we can change leaf quantity
while holding arrangement fixed, and change arrangement while holding quantity fixed. The
collinearity that makes attribution impossible in observational data is absent from a designed
set of simulations, because the experimenter, not the forest, decides which combinations are
run.

### 1.3.2 The MuSICA model

MuSICA is a multilayer soil-vegetation-atmosphere transfer model that solves energy, water, and
carbon exchange through a vertically discretised canopy [@ogeeMuSICACO2Water2003]. Radiative transfer is computed layer by layer, and separately for sunlit and shaded foliage. The leaf energy balance and stomatal conductance are solved at each level, and turbulent transfer links the layers to the air above. Soil water and heat are treated in a coupled multilayer column. The model was developed and evaluated at European forest flux sites across timescales from hourly to yearly [@ogeeMuSICACO2Water2003; @ogeePartitioningNetEcosystem2003]. It has already been coupled to LiDAR-derived structure to study sub-canopy microclimate in conifer stands [@bouwenInteractionsEntreStructure].

![**Figure 1.7.** What the MuSICA model contains. **(A)** The discretisation: several vegetation layers, several species or age classes, several leaf types within a layer, sunlit and shaded, wet and dry, and several soil and litter layers with their own root profiles. **(B)** The processes solved on that discretisation: radiative transfer, rain and snow interception, photosynthesis and respiration, transpiration and evaporation, turbulent transfer, and the coupled transport of water and heat in the soil. Sub-canopy microclimate is an output of the turbulent and radiative terms rather than a separate module, which is what makes the model answerable to a temperature logger. That study is the closest existing work to this one. It established that a multilayer canopy
model can reproduce the ordering of sub-canopy conditions across stands of contrasting density.
It also established that the meteorological forcing must itself be corrected for canopy
structure before scenarios of differing structure can be compared. For lack of measured
vertical profiles, the distribution of foliage was described throughout by a parametric beta
function. That unimodal shape cannot represent the stratified canopies produced by partial
harvesting, and its use was judged to have contributed to an underestimation of the simulated
buffering. Its closing recommendation was to feed measured LiDAR-derived profiles of plant area
density into the model in place of that function.

Three things remain open. The measured profile has not been substituted for the assumed one, so the effect of the vertical arrangement itself has not been isolated. A profile held to a single parametric family cannot be contrasted against an alternative arrangement at matched leaf area and height. The work was conducted in evergreen conifer stands, where the seasonal cycle of
foliage and the geometry of interception both differ from a temperate deciduous canopy. And
nothing in it addresses how the structural input might be obtained beyond the footprint where
the LiDAR was flown, the separate problem taken up in Section 1.3.3.

Three properties make MuSICA the appropriate tool for the question of Section 1.2.2. The first
is that it is multilayer. A vertical profile of leaf area density is a native input, whereas a
big-leaf scheme collapses the canopy into a single effective surface. If the arrangement of
foliage is to be tested against its quantity, the model must be able to see both. MuSICA
belongs to a small family of canopy schemes with this property: multilayer biophysical models
developed for temperate deciduous stands [@baldocchiHowEnvironmentCanopy2002],
three-dimensional radiation and gas-exchange models [@sinoquetRATPModelSimulating2001], and
integrated soil-canopy radiative and energy-balance models
[@vandertolIntegratedModelSoilcanopy2009]. Multilayer canopy parameterisations are now being
introduced into land surface models as well [@bonanModelingCanopyinducedTurbulence2018;
@bonanModelingStomatalConductance2014]. Empirical microclimate downscalers take canopy cover or
a height metric as a scalar predictor and could not accept a profile even if one were
available.

![**Figure 1.8.** Schematic: why the canopy scheme has to be multilayer. What matters here is not what
the model computes but what it can accept. In (a) a vertical profile of leaf area density is a native
input: the canopy is discretised into layers, each holding sunlit and shaded foliage with its own

is lost on the way in, and the question of Section 1.2.2 cannot be posed at all. Layer count and

The second is that the coupling between structure and temperature is physical rather than
empirical. No transfer function has to be fitted between the two, which removes the attribution problem of Section 1.3.1. A sensitivity computed by perturbing an input of a physical model is a property of the physics, not an estimate contaminated by the covariance structure of a training set.

The third is that the canopy inputs are explicit and separable. Leaf area, canopy height, cover, and the shape of the vertical profile enter as distinct quantities. That is what makes controlled perturbation possible, and what allows the vertical profile to be contrasted against a leaf-area- and height-preserving uniform alternative.

One limitation bounds everything that follows. MuSICA is a one-dimensional column, so lateral
advection, edge effects, and sub-pixel canopy gaps lie outside its representation. No such
model can be expected to reproduce a plot whose surroundings differ from that column, for
instance one containing a gap the mean structural input does not resolve. The argument
developed here therefore rests on relative comparisons between plots rather than on absolute
temperatures.

### 1.3.3 From plot to landscape

Mechanistic canopy models are plot-scale by construction. They are one-dimensional columns: they
require, for every location simulated, a complete structural description of the canopy above that
point together with the meteorological forcing above it. Running one such column is cheap.

The structural inputs are the bottleneck. Measuring LAI in the field, whether by hemispherical
photography, optical plant-canopy analysers, or destructive sampling, is slow, weather-dependent, and
subject to its own methodological uncertainty [@weissReviewMethodsSitu2004;
@chenEvaluationHemisphericalPhotography1991; @holstMeasuringModellingPlant2004]. Different indirect
methods disagree with one another even when applied at the same sites, and the disagreement is
largest where it matters most, in the understory layer [@georgeMethodComparisonIndirect2021].
Automated processing has improved reproducibility [@brownHemiPyPythonModule2023], but it has not changed the arithmetic. Measuring a vertical LAD profile in the field is harder still, and a campaign of that kind yields tens of plots, occasionally hundreds.

A landscape contains millions of model pixels. There is no route from plot-scale mechanism to
landscape-scale microclimate map that does not pass through a structural dataset acquired remotely,
continuously, and at fine spatial resolution.

Choosing that resolution is a modelling decision, not a technical one. A one-dimensional column
assumes that the canopy above the simulated point is horizontally uniform over the area it
represents. The pixel must therefore be small enough for that assumption to hold and large
enough for the structural retrieval to be reliable. Too coarse, and a single column has to
stand for a mixture of gaps and closed canopy whose average behaves like neither
[@garriguesInfluenceLandscapeSpatial2006]. Too fine, and the retrieval noise of the structural
input dominates the structural signal. Attributing a thermal effect to canopy structure calls
for footprints large enough to define a stable vertical profile, while comparing satellite
against airborne retrievals is constrained to the 10 m grid the satellite imposes. Both sit
within the range over which sub-canopy temperature has been shown to vary in temperate stands
[@zellwegerAdvancesMicroclimateEcology2019; @mengeImpactsForestManagement2023]. Which
instrument can populate that grid, and what each one costs in structural fidelity or temporal
coverage, is the subject of the next section.

### 1.4.1 Airborne LiDAR

Airborne laser scanning (ALS) is the only operational technique that resolves the interior of a
forest canopy over an area. Laser pulses penetrate gaps in the foliage and return a three-dimensional point cloud. The ground surface is identified and the cloud normalised to height above ground. The vertical gap fraction is then computed layer by layer and inverted, through a Beer-Lambert formulation, into a profile of plant area density and an integrated LAI [@bouvier7_GeneralizingPredictiveModels2015; @richardsonModelingApproachesEstimate2009; @zhengRetrievalEffectiveLeaf2013]. The approach has been established for two decades and validated across forest types and sensors [@lefskyLidarBlackwellScienceLtdRemote2002; @morsdorfEstimationLAIFractional2006; @chenUsingLidarEffective2004; @kwakEstimationEffectivePlant2010; @peduzziEstimatingLeafArea2012]. Reconstructing full LAD profiles rather than integrated totals is now routine [@linRetrievalEffectiveLeaf2016; @kamoskeLeafAreaDensity2019; @arnqvistRobustProcessingAirborne2020].

The retrieval is not parameter-free, and its assumptions matter for this thesis. The conversion
from gap fraction to leaf area rests on an extinction coefficient that encodes leaf angle
distribution and is commonly fixed at a nominal value. It also assumes a random foliage distribution, which real canopies violate through clumping [@huUsingAirborneLaser2018]. The returns come from wood as well as leaves, so the retrieval is a plant area index unless the two are separated, which matched leaf-on and leaf-off acquisitions can do in deciduous stands [@zhuVoxelMatchingMethod2020].
Acquisition geometry leaves its own imprint, since flying height, scan angle, and pulse density
all affect the signal's penetration depth. Uncertainty in voxel-scale plant area density
therefore has to be propagated rather than ignored
[@pimont5_EstimatorsConfidenceIntervals2018], and canopy height models derived from the same
cloud carry their own processing choices [@khosravipourGeneratingPitfreeCanopy2014]. A LiDAR
LAI is a model-based estimate resting on stated assumptions, not a direct measurement. That
matters as soon as such an estimate is set against an optical one, because neither side of the
comparison is a reference truth.

ALS also delivers more than leaf area. The same point cloud yields a digital terrain model, a
canopy height model, fractional cover, and the full suite of height and density metrics on
which forest inventory modelling rests [@khosravipourGeneratingPitfreeCanopy2014;
@bouvier7_GeneralizingPredictiveModels2015]. An ALS survey is best read as a structural
description of a stand rather than as a leaf-area sensor. Canopy height is easier to retrieve
than leaf area and is correlated with it, which makes it the fallback wherever a full profile
is unavailable. Terrestrial and portable scanning reconstruct leaf area density at the scale of
individual crowns [@hosoiVoxelBased3DModeling2006; @liEstimatingLeafArea2017], but they cover
plots rather than landscapes.

ALS-derived structure has since become a standard input to microclimate studies, whether to map
buffering directly [@grilUsingAirborneLiDAR2023; @vandewieleMappingSpatialMicroclimate2023], to
constrain it jointly with topography [@juckerCanopyStructureTopography2018], or to explain the
thermal shelters used by wildlife [@melinMooseLcesAlces2014]. For the question of Section 1.2.2 its
value is specific: it is the sensor that delivers the vertical arrangement, not a proxy for it.

Its spatio-temporal signature is the mirror image of that strength. ALS is acquired by aircraft
campaign, which makes it expensive, spatially bounded by the flight plan, and, in practice,
single-date. A survey describes the canopy as it was on one summer afternoon. It says nothing
about budburst, senescence, or the following year, and it does not extend one metre beyond its
footprint.

The extinction coefficient and the assumption of randomly placed foliage are annotated on the step that introduces them. ### 1.4.2 Optical satellite imagery

Sentinel-2 occupies the opposite corner. The two-satellite constellation images every land surface every five days in thirteen spectral bands, four of them delivered at 10 m and six at 20 m. Three of the 20 m bands sit on the red edge, where they are sensitive to canopy chlorophyll and leaf area [@druschSentinel2ESAsOptical2012; @delegidoEvaluationSentinel2RedEdge2011]. The mission was designed
for operational vegetation monitoring, and leaf area index is one of its standard biophysical
products. The mixed resolution is not a detail for forest work. Restricting an inversion to the 10 m bands gains spatial detail and leaves the red-edge and shortwave infrared information unused, while including the 20 m bands does the reverse.

Chapter 2 restricts the inversion to the 10 m bands, on the grounds that spatial detail matters in heterogeneous canopy, and states the relevance of the 20 m bands as an open
question.

Retrieval proceeds by inverting a canopy radiative-transfer model against the observed
reflectance. The standard model couples a leaf optical model with a canopy scattering scheme,
giving PROSAIL [@jacquemoudPROSPECT+SAILModelsReview2009; @verhoefLightScatteringLeaf1984;
@feretPROSPECTDModelingLeaf2017; @feretProsailPROSAILLeaf2024]. Because inverting it directly
is ill-posed, in the sense that different parameter combinations produce nearly identical
spectra, the inversion is regularised by prior information on the parameter ranges
[@combalRetrievalCanopyBiophysical2002]. The neural network distributed with the Sentinel
toolbox is trained on a simulated database built from prescribed parameter distributions
[@weissS2ToolBoxLevel22020], and hybrid inversion trains a machine-learning regressor on a
comparable look-up table [@verrelstMachineLearningRegression2012;
@verrelstExperimentalSentinel2LAI2015; @verrelstQuantifyingVegetationBiophysical2019]. Both
have been applied to forests [@chrysafisRetrievalLeafArea2020; @fernandesNotJustPretty2024] and
validated against field data [@brownValidationBaselineModified2021].

Turning that into a usable time series takes an operational chain. Top-of-atmosphere
reflectance must be atmospherically corrected, clouds and their shadows masked, and observation
and illumination geometry recorded, since the inversion depends on them
[@feretPreprocS2PreprocessingSentinel22024]. Cloud cover in temperate Europe removes a large share of the nominal five-day revisit, so the surviving observations are irregularly spaced. A continuous trajectory has to be reconstructed by gap-filling and smoothing before it can force anything [@atzbergerTimeSeriesMonitoring2011; @liangUsingEnhancedGapFilling2023]. The
satellite therefore delivers a smoothed estimate of a seasonal trajectory rather than a set of
measurements. That trajectory is its advantage over an airborne survey, because the timing of
leaf-out and senescence is observed per pixel rather than assumed
[@cotrina-sanchezPhenologyEuropeanForests2026]. It is not free of artifacts. Surface reflectance is directional, and the sun and view geometry of an acquisition change through the season independently of the canopy [@maignanBidirectionalReflectanceEarth2004; @breonAnalysisHotSpot2002]. A retrieved trajectory can therefore drift after the summer solstice for reasons unrelated to leaf loss. Chapter 3 therefore treats the seasonality of Sentinel-2 as a
signal to be tested rather than as a property to be assumed.

Four constraints qualify it, and together they define the gap set out in Section 1.5.2. The
first is geometric: the signal is a two-dimensional reflectance measured from above, so it
carries information about the upper canopy and only indirectly about what lies beneath. The
second is the saturation that follows from it. Sensitivity declines as leaf area accumulates
and is exhausted somewhere above five, the exact point depending on the retrieval, the index,
and the canopy [@gaoEvaluatingSaturationEffect2023;
@aklilutesfayeEvaluationSaturationProperty2021]. Closed temperate forest canopies are at or beyond that limit for much of the growing season. The consequence is a compression rather than an abrupt ceiling: differences in leaf area between dense stands are progressively under-expressed in the reflectance before they disappear from it. The third is the prior information that regularises the inversion. Operational parameter ranges are tuned for crops, and transferring them to closed forest canopies biases the retrieval, while correcting the bias trades against variance [@fernandesEvidenceBiasvarianceTrade2024]. The
fourth is spatial heterogeneity: because the reflectance-to-LAI relationship is non-linear, a
pixel containing a mixture of canopy heights or densities does not return the average of its
parts [@garriguesInfluenceLandscapeSpatial2006; @maImpactSpatialLAI2008]. Continuous temporal
coverage therefore comes at the cost of structural depth, and the cost is largest exactly where
the canopy is densest and most heterogeneous.

## 1.5 Identifying the scientific gap

### 1.5.1 The attribution gap

The first gap is one of causal attribution. Canopy structural variables covary strongly in real
stands, so correlative microclimate models cannot assign the buffering effect to any one of
them. Coefficients are unstable, smooth terms are concurve, and machine-learning importance
measures redistribute themselves among correlated predictors in ways that depend on the
algorithm [@dominiciUseGeneralizedAdditive2002; @nicodemusBehaviourRandomForest2010;
@gregoruttiCorrelationVariableImportance2017]. The question posed in Section 1.2.2, whether the
quantity of foliage or its vertical arrangement governs sub-canopy buffering, therefore remains
open despite an extensive literature on both sides [@kovacsStandStructuralDrivers2017;
@ehbrechtEffectsStructuralHeterogeneity2019].

The gap is methodological. More field data collected along the same natural gradient will not
close it, because the gradient itself confounds the predictors. Closing it requires
observations in which the structural variables move independently of one another. No forest offers such observations, so they have to be generated by a mechanistic model driven by controlled combinations of canopy inputs. The perturbations are applied around each plot's own measured canopy, so every simulated configuration stays close to one that occurs in the field.

Simulation in place of observation raises one objection: a regression whose coefficients are
unidentifiable has been traded for a model whose output cannot be checked. Three properties
answer it. First, the model's parameters are physical and are not fitted to the response being
explained, so the answer cannot enter through a calibration step. The sensitivity of simulated
temperature to leaf area follows from radiative and turbulent transfer. Second, the
perturbations are small and centred on each plot's measured canopy, so the physics is never
extrapolated into regimes where it has not been evaluated. Third, the model's ability to
reproduce the observed between-plot buffering is established separately against the logger
network. The attribution is therefore made with a model whose behaviour on real stands has been
characterised independently of it. No design gives a direct empirical test of the attribution
itself, but the physics it rests on can be tested independently of the gradient at issue,
unlike a fitted coefficient.

In real stands the structural variables covary, so the observations occupy a narrow band of the space they span; a regression fitted to that band estimates the direction along it and leaves the direction across it unidentified, and further sampling along the same gradient adds no information about the second direction. ### 1.5.2 Optical versus structural LAI

The second gap concerns the measurement of leaf area itself. Airborne LiDAR and Sentinel-2 both
deliver a quantity called LAI, but they measure different physical things. LiDAR integrates
intercepted returns through the whole canopy column. The optical retrieval inverts a
reflectance signal that originates mostly near the top [@garaAccurateModellingCanopy2019] and
stops responding once the canopy is dense [@baretPotentialsLimitsVegetation1991;
@gaoEvaluatingSaturationEffect2023]. The two are known to disagree, and the disagreement is
expected to grow with canopy density, but its magnitude and its drivers have not been
characterised jointly for temperate deciduous forest.

Three candidates contribute, and they are rarely assessed together. Optical attenuation limits
the canopy depth that contributes to satellite reflectance, so the satellite may describe only
the upper part of the column that the LiDAR integrates in full. The radiative-transfer
inversion supplies prior ranges tuned for crops rather than for forest canopies, and those
priors propagate into the retrieved leaf area [@combalRetrievalCanopyBiophysical2002;
@fernandesEvidenceBiasvarianceTrade2024]. Horizontal heterogeneity within a satellite pixel,
which combines variation in canopy height with variation in the terrain below, strains the one-dimensional assumption the inversion rests on, and does so non-linearly
[@garriguesInfluenceLandscapeSpatial2006].

The question is one of inter-sensor consistency, a prerequisite rather than a microclimate
claim. No ground-based LAI measurement coincident with the airborne and satellite acquisitions
exists at these sites, so nothing external can decide between the two retrievals and the target
cannot be absolute accuracy. The reachable target is which part of the canopy the satellite
number describes, and it has to be settled before a satellite LAI forces a microclimate model.

Two caveats condition everything downstream. The first is domain. Consistency between two sensors holds over the structural domain on which it was established. A depth-based comparison has to restrict itself to canopy deep and closed enough to have an interior at all. A search over successive canopy depths cannot converge on stands shallower than the depths it scans, so pixels below a minimum canopy height are excluded. A high fractional-cover threshold keeps gaps and edges out of the pixel. Any agreement reached on that restricted
domain is silent about short, open stands, where an optical retrieval behaves differently.

The second caveat is that better agreement in leaf area does not propagate into better
simulated microclimate. A mechanistic canopy model responds non-linearly to leaf area, steeply
in open canopy and with strong saturation once the canopy closes. The same reduction in leaf-area error is therefore worth different amounts depending on where along that curve it applies.
A retrieval tuned to minimise leaf-area discrepancy over one part of the range can be a worse driver of the microclimate over another. It can even reverse the ordering of plots, if the tuning compresses the gradient the model relies on. Which of the two objectives a correction improves,
leaf-area accuracy or microclimate skill, is an empirical question.

### 1.5.3 The spatio-temporal trade-off

The third gap follows from the first two. ALS resolves three-dimensional structure but is
acquired once and over a limited footprint. Sentinel-2 is repeated and spatially complete, but
optical, two-dimensional and saturating. Spaceborne LiDAR samples the vertical profile
repeatedly, in sparse footprints [@dubayahGlobalEcosystemDynamics2020]. No single sensor
currently delivers fine three-dimensional structure and temporal dynamics together.

Driving a mechanistic model beyond the plots where LiDAR happens to exist therefore forces a
compromise between structural fidelity and coverage. That compromise has usually been made
implicitly, by using whichever product was available, and evaluated by pooling all plots into a
single accuracy statistic. Neither the choice nor the evaluation has been examined against the
regime dependence that Sections 1.4.2 and 1.5.2 predict. If the optical signal fails
specifically where the canopy is dense, a single pooled conclusion on which sensor is better
can conceal two opposite conclusions in the two halves of the density gradient. Which
compromise is right, and whether it is the same compromise everywhere in a landscape, has not
been established.

The three gaps have to be addressed in order. The structural dimension the microclimate
responds to defines what a sensor must measure. Whether Sentinel-2 measures it depends on the
part of the canopy the optical signal describes. The choice of forcing product can only be
judged once both are known, and the order of the chapters follows the order of the gaps.

## 1.6 Research questions and objectives

**RQ1 (Chapter 1).** *Which dimension of canopy structure governs sub-canopy ΔTmax buffering, leaf
quantity or vertical arrangement, and can a mechanistic model attribute it despite the collinearity
of structural variables?*

The objective is to couple ALS-derived canopy structure (LAI, fCover, *H*max, and the LAD
profile) to MuSICA and to run a controlled trait-perturbation sensitivity analysis. Each
structural variable is moved by a small, native step around every plot's own canopy, and the
measured vertical profile is contrasted against a leaf-area- and height-preserving uniform
profile. Perturbing each plot around its own operating point gives the local response of a real
canopy rather than an extrapolation. The resulting effects are stratified by structural
archetype, so the answer may differ between open and dense canopy instead of being averaged
into one conclusion. The simulated buffering is validated against an in-situ network of 53
temperature loggers. Two hypotheses are posed. The first is that vertical structure modulates
radiative and turbulent transfer enough for the LAD profile to change simulated ΔTmax
measurably. The second is that, at fixed leaf area and height, foliage concentrated high in the
canopy attenuates the diurnal cycle more strongly than foliage spread through the column.

**RQ2 (Chapter 2).** *Can Sentinel-2 optical LAI be reconciled with LiDAR-derived structural LAI, or
does optical saturation decouple them?*

The objective is to intercompare the two retrievals across three temperate deciduous forests and to assess the three candidate drivers of their discrepancy identified in Section 1.5.2. These are the effective canopy depth contributing to the satellite reflectance, the parameterisation of the PROSAIL inversion, and horizontal heterogeneity of the canopy surface within the satellite pixel. Each correction is applied on top of the previous one, so their individual contributions
can be read. The target is inter-sensor consistency, not absolute accuracy, since no coincident
field measurement of LAI is available to decide between the two sensors. The output is a
quantified statement of how well, and under what canopy conditions, the two retrievals can be
brought into agreement, to be carried forward as a condition of use.

**RQ3 (Chapter 3).** *Which remotely sensed leaf area should force a mechanistic microclimate model, does the answer depend on canopy archetype, and can the seasonality of Sentinel-2 track the microclimate without losing the accuracy of LiDAR?*

The objective is to force MuSICA with four leaf-area scenarios, holding canopy height and the
shape of the vertical profile at their LiDAR values, so that the design isolates the effect of
the leaf-area source. The first scenario holds the LiDAR leaf area fixed. The second and third
use the seasonal Sentinel-2 retrieval, first as delivered and then forest-tuned. The fourth
applies the Sentinel-2 seasonality to the LiDAR magnitude.

One contrast organises the chapter: a satellite leaf area that describes the upper canopy
against a LiDAR leaf area that integrates the whole column. The deficit expected in dense
canopy is the leaf area lying below the depth the optical signal reaches. The archetypes of
Chapter 1 separate the canopies where that deficit matters from those where it does not. The
scenarios are scored against the logger network, archetype by archetype, on a within-plot
paired sensitivity test and a direct validation, with the coupling slope as primary metric and
the daytime offset as secondary. We hypothesise that no single product forces the microclimate
best in every archetype: seasonal Sentinel-2 should rank the plots where it does not saturate,
and where it saturates its magnitude should come from LiDAR.

: **Table 1.3.** What each research chapter uses. The progression is one of
constraint rather than of scale. Chapter 1 establishes which structural
variable the microclimate responds to. Chapter 2 asks whether a satellite can
measure that variable. Chapter 3 asks which retrieval should then drive the
model. Only Chapter 2 leaves the single site, and it does so to test whether
the optical discrepancy is general rather than local.

**Chapter 1** answers RQ1. ALS-derived structure from a lowland sessile oak forest drives
MuSICA at 400 design plots, stratified into four structural archetypes from open to dense. Each
structural variable is perturbed around every plot's own canopy, so its contribution to
simulated ΔTmax is isolated by construction rather than inferred from correlated observations.
The answer depends on canopy density, which reconciles the conflicting literature of Section
1.2.2. Leaf quantity leads at both ends of the gradient, and its effect steepens as the canopy
closes. The vertical profile matters only as an upper bound. The contrast between the real profile and a uniform one of equal leaf area and height gives the largest single effect in intermediate canopy, and real canopies barely span it. A stand-level validation against the 53
loggers establishes the model's credibility rather than testing the per-plot sensitivities,
since the perturbation design points are not the logger plots. The model reproduces the
between-plot buffering ranking well, carries a warm bias on the absolute offset, and compresses
the micro-macro slope towards unity. Because the microclimate responds to leaf quantity, the
next question is whether a satellite can measure it.

**Chapter 2** answers RQ2. Sentinel-2 and LiDAR LAI are intercompared across three temperate
deciduous forests, a montane beech stand and two lowland oak-dominated ones, and the three
candidate drivers of their discrepancy are addressed in sequence. Satellite LAI agrees best
with the LiDAR estimate when the latter is restricted to the upper 6 to 10 m of the canopy.
That depth is empirical, the one at which the two sensors are most consistent, not a measured
optical penetration. Adapting the PROSAIL parameter ranges to forest canopies then improves
agreement unevenly, and most of that gain survives when the LiDAR-derived priors are replaced
by LiDAR-free ones. Residual discrepancies remain largest where the canopy surface is
horizontally heterogeneous within the pixel, so vertical thickness and horizontal heterogeneity
have to be accounted for jointly. What improves is inter-sensor consistency, not accuracy
against a field truth, and it holds only on the closed, tall canopy the comparison was posed
on. Chapter 3 reads this as a statement of where the optical signal can be trusted, not as a
correction to apply everywhere.

**Chapter 3** answers RQ3. MuSICA is forced at the 53 logger plots, not over a landscape, with
four leaf-area scenarios, and the best product depends on the canopy archetype. In mid-density
canopy the raw seasonal Sentinel-2 retrieval ranks the plots better than LiDAR. In dense canopy
Sentinel-2 saturates, and the ranking is held only by products carrying the LiDAR magnitude. In
the open gaps Sentinel-2 retrieves a leaf area that the LiDAR point cloud shows is not there.
The mechanism is the one diagnosed in Chapter 2. Most of the leaf area of a dense canopy lies below the effective optical depth, so the Sentinel-2 deficit grows with the fraction buried below it. Section 1.5.2 anticipated the consequence. The forest-tuned retrieval
that is most consistent with LiDAR drives the dense microclimate worse than the raw one,
because a tuning that reduces the mean discrepancy flattens the between-plot contrast the model
needs. The summer window is a weak test of the seasonal shape, which is almost flat on a
full-leaf plateau, so what the combination adds can only be decided at leaf-out and senescence.

This thesis does not produce a wall-to-wall microclimate map. It establishes what a map would have to be built on. That is which structural dimension the temperature responds to, what a satellite can see of it, and which product should drive the model in which archetype.
Mapping stays out of reach for two reasons established in the chapters. The forcing still
requires LiDAR structure wherever the canopy is dense, so coverage is bounded by the flight
footprint. And a one-dimensional column cannot represent the gaps, edges, and lateral exchanges
a landscape is made of.

The microclimate work rests on a single lowland oak site and on the summer 2021 window, so the
archetype-dependent conclusion of Chapter 3 will require testing elsewhere. The loggers are not
spatially independent, which makes its confidence intervals optimistic. Within summer a
temporally resolved satellite series adds nothing, because leaf area is on its phenological
plateau and there is no timing left to resolve. Outside summer the chapters do not test the
question, and the General Discussion draws on the later logger archive for perspectives only.
On the optical side no ground LAI coincident with the acquisitions is available, so Chapter 2
establishes consistency rather than accuracy.

Two avenues are left open and treated in the General Discussion. The first is spaceborne LiDAR.
GEDI and the global canopy-height products derived from it promise three-dimensional structure with satellite coverage [@dubayahGlobalEcosystemDynamics2020; @langHighresolutionCanopyHeight2022; @schwartzFORMSForestMultiple2023]. Plant area index products are being validated from the same instrument [@brownStage1Validation2023], and fusion frameworks with Sentinel-2 are emerging for wall-to-wall retrieval [@jiaGEDISentinel2IntegrationFramework2026]. Its sampling geometry and geolocation uncertainty
remain too coarse for plot-scale mechanistic microclimate modelling
[@schleichImprovingGEDIFootprint2023].

The second avenue is dimensional. Forest edges are warmer than interiors
[@reekForestEdgesAre2025], and the residuals of Chapter 1 are structured by the sub-pixel gaps
a one-dimensional column cannot see. Heterogeneity- and edge-aware formulations that couple a
canopy scheme to a three-dimensional atmospheric solver relax that assumption [REF: Grulois
2026, coupled ARPS-MuSICA thesis; @vandewalleForEdgeClimV103D2026]. The archetype-dependent
forcing of Chapter 3 can then be evaluated where lateral fluxes matter.
